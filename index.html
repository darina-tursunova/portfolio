Module 1 · Task 1 of 10

Interview with a teacher

Assignment

Interview with… Think of 5 questions on the use of the group work. Ask these questions (choose the really working teachers), present in the form of the report.

Aim

To learn from an experienced teacher how group work really goes in a language classroom: how groups are formed, what goes wrong and how the result is evaluated.

My work

I prepared five questions. Each of them is connected with one part of the lecture: the place of group work in a lesson, forming groups and roles, drawbacks, evaluation and examples.

I did not want a list of textbook answers, so I asked about real lessons: what was done, with which forms and what went wrong. Below is the report on our talk. I translated the answers from Russian.

  • 10–15 minutes of group work in almost every lesson
  • 4–5 pupils in a mixed group
  • 4 changing roles: speaker, secretary, timekeeper, language keeper
  • 3 views in evaluation: teacher, other groups, the pupil

The rules hung on the board, and when somebody broke one, it was enough to point at it.

  1. How often did you use group work in your lessons, and at which stage of the lesson?

    Group work was in almost every lesson and usually took 10–15 minutes. In the 5th–7th forms it was mostly at the stage of working with a text: the groups read, answered questions and looked for the necessary information. In the 9th form it was at the stage of consolidation, in the form of a quest, where the groups moved from station to station and did tasks on the topic we had studied. At the end of the lesson group work helped to sum up and to reflect.

  2. How did you form the groups and share the roles?

    To share the roles in the 9th–11th forms we used short leadership tests. They showed who was ready to lead and who would do better as an executor or a secretary. After that we made mixed groups of four or five pupils, and each group had a pupil with leadership qualities. The roles of the speaker, the secretary, the timekeeper and the “language keeper” changed from task to task, so that pupils tried different positions and not only the usual one.

  3. What difficulties did you meet in group work, and how did you solve them?

    The main difficulties were passive pupils, dominating leaders and noise, especially in the senior forms. So in the 10th–11th forms the pupils and the teacher wrote the rules of group work together at the very beginning: speak in turn, listen to each other, play your role and speak the foreign language. The rules hung on the board, and when somebody broke one, it was enough to point at it. Passive pupils got a specific part of the task. Active leaders became timekeepers, so that they organised the work and did not do everything themselves.

  4. How did you evaluate the work of the group and the contribution of each pupil?

    In the 9th form the quest was assessed with rubrics, which the pupils received in advance: understanding of the task, accuracy of the language, teamwork and presentation of the result. The work of a group was evaluated by the teacher and by the other groups. The contribution of each pupil was seen through observation and self-evaluation inside the group, so it was clear who really took part. After group work there was a short individual task to check that everyone had understood the topic.

  5. Which group task worked best in your French or Kazakh lessons, and what would you advise a beginning teacher?

    The best one was the quest in the 9th form. The groups went through stations with reading, vocabulary and speaking tasks and got a clue to the next stage. The pupils were involved because the task had a goal and an element of competition, and the rubrics made the assessment fair and clear. My advice to a beginning teacher is to agree on the rules of group work with the class in advance and to give clear instructions with a time limit. It is also useful to start with simple tasks, for example work with a text in the junior forms, and to move step by step to complex formats like quests.

My conclusions

  • Group work is a regular short part of a lesson (10–15 minutes), not a rare event.
  • Two of the typical problems are the same as in the lecture: dominating pupils and passive (“lazy”) pupils. The teacher added a third one, noise. They are solved before the task starts, with rules and roles.
  • Fair evaluation needs three views: the teacher, the other groups and the pupil.

What I learned

While preparing the questions I learned to ask about practice, not about theory: a good question starts with “how” and asks for an example. I also saw that five questions are enough if each one covers a different side of group work.

The answers showed me that the other tasks of this portfolio are connected in real practice: rules, roles, leadership tests, a quest and rubrics work together in one lesson. The advice I will follow first is to agree on the rules with the class before the first group task.

Module 1 · Task 2 of 10

Rules for the group work

Assignment

Elaborate the rules for the group work for the pupils of the 10th–11th form (7–10 rules).

Aim

To give senior pupils a short and clear code that makes group work fair and productive, and to practise presenting classroom rules in a visual form.

My work

I wrote eight rules and hid them in the word TEAMWORK, so that pupils can remember them easily. Each letter opens one rule.

  1. T

    Take a role

    Everyone has a job: manager, writer, speaker or critic. We change roles next time.

  2. E

    Everyone contributes

    No passengers. Each of us gives at least one idea before the group decides.

  3. A

    Argue with ideas, not with people

    We can disagree, but politely and with a reason: “I see it differently, because…”

  4. M

    Mind the time

    The manager watches the clock. We plan the minutes and finish on time.

  5. W

    We speak English

    The task is our chance to practise. If we slip into another language, we switch back.

  6. O

    One voice at a time

    We listen to the end and do not interrupt. Quiet members are asked first.

  7. R

    Respect every opinion

    No idea is laughed at. A strange idea today can be the best one tomorrow.

  8. K

    Keep the goal in sight

    We check the result against the task and finish with a short self- and peer evaluation.

What I learned

While writing the rules I understood that most problems of group work, such as the dominating pupil or the lazy one, can be prevented before the task starts. Rules work better when they are short, positive and agreed with the class, so I would present my eight rules as a draft and let the pupils change them. I also practised turning a list into a visual product that senior pupils will actually read.

Module 2 · Task 3 of 10

Quest on the critical thinking

Assignment

Make a quest for critical thinking. Choose the topic: Travelling, History, Famous people or Kazakhstan. Elaborate tasks mediated online, from the easiest one to the most difficult.

My choice: the topic “Kazakhstan”, for the 9th form (level A2+/B1), one lesson of 45 minutes.

Aim

To make a quest of five online tasks about Kazakhstan that grow in difficulty and train pupils to ask questions, to give arguments and to make a balanced decision.

My work

Kazakhstan: the Great Steppe Quest

9th form (14–15 years old) · level A2+/B1 · 45 minutes · one device for each pair

Welcome to the Great Steppe Quest. A guest from another country is coming to Kazakhstan, and you are the travel bloggers who must get ready. Do five tasks, from the easiest to the most difficult, check every fact and plan the best three-day trip.

The quest follows the three phases of the lesson from the lecture (Temple, Meredith and Steele, 1997): challenge, comprehension and reflection. It uses the techniques of the lecture: True/False statements with prediction, Bloom’s chamomile, “Fact or Opinion”, INSERT marks and “Tour guide for an alien”. I checked all the links on 23 November 2026. They opened without a login.

Overview of the quest

The tasks take 42 minutes. The last 3 minutes are a closing reflection: pupils return to their table from Task 1, tick the predictions that were right and finish the sentence “I thought that ..., but now I know that ...”.

How the difficulty grows. In Task 1 pupils recognise and check single facts. In Task 2 they answer questions from simple to practical. In Task 3 they judge statements. In Task 4 they analyse two texts and compare them. In Task 5 they create a new product and defend it. The support becomes smaller at the same time: first a ready table and individual work, then pair work with given questions, then group work with a free choice.

Task 1. True or False Prediction: Predict first, then open the page and check.

Link: The Republic of Kazakhstan (opens in a new tab) (official website of the President of the Republic of Kazakhstan)

Challenge · individual · 4 minutes · difficulty 1

Instruction for pupils:

  1. Do not open the link yet. Read the eight statements about Kazakhstan. In the column “My prediction” write T (true) or F (false). You have about 2 minutes.
  2. Now open the link. Use “Find on page” (Ctrl+F), type the word ninth and read that paragraph and the next one. In the column “The page says” write T or F.
  3. Correct each false statement: cross out the wrong word or number and write the right one. Compare your answers with your partner.
  4. Keep your table. We will come back to it at the end of the lesson.
  1. Kazakhstan is the ninth largest country in the world.
  2. Kazakhstan has land borders with six countries.
  3. The longest border of Kazakhstan is the border with Russia.
  4. Kazakhstan is the largest landlocked country in the world (a landlocked country has no coast on an ocean).
  5. Most of Kazakhstan is steppe.
  6. There are fewer than 1,000 lakes in Kazakhstan.
  7. The Aral Sea is divided between two countries: Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan.
  8. Kazakhstan has a mild sea climate because it is close to the ocean.

Product: a table with the eight statements and three columns: “My prediction”, “The page says” and the correction of each false statement.

Key for the teacher
  1. True.
  2. False. There are five neighbours: Russia, China, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan.
  3. True. The border with Russia is 7,591 km long.
  4. True.
  5. False. Steppe is 26 %, desert is 44 % and semi-desert is 14 %.
  6. False. There are about 48,000 lakes.
  7. True.
  8. False. The climate is continental, because the country is far from the oceans.

Success criteria: every statement has a prediction and a checked answer; the four false statements are corrected in writing; the pupil can show the sentence on the page that proves each answer. I praise careful checking, not lucky predictions.

Task 2. Bloom’s Chamomile: Watch the video and answer six questions.

Link: Saryarka - Steppe and Lakes of Northern Kazakhstan (UNESCO/NHK) (opens in a new tab) (official YouTube channel of UNESCO, 2 minutes 51 seconds)

Comprehension · pairs · 8 minutes · difficulty 2

Instruction for pupils:

  1. Open the link. Before you press Play, read the title of the video. Tell your partner what you expect to see.
  2. Before you watch, open Settings, choose “Audio track: English (original)” and turn on English subtitles. Then watch the video.
  3. Under the video click “...more” and read the short text.
  4. With your partner answer the six questions, one for each petal of Bloom’s chamomile. Write short answers of one or two sentences. Questions 4, 5 and 6 have no single right answer: give your reason.

Useful words:

  • wetlands: land with a lot of water (lakes and marshes)
  • migratory birds: birds that fly to another part of the world every year
  • stopover: a short stop on a long journey
  • nature reserve: a place where animals and plants are protected
  • poaching: illegal hunting
  1. Simple question. Where is Saryarka, and which two kinds of landscape are protected there?
  2. Clarifying question. Do I understand correctly that Saryarka is one big national park?
  3. Interpreting question. Why do so many birds from Africa, Europe and South Asia stop at these lakes?
  4. Creative question. What would happen to the birds if the lakes of Saryarka dried up?
  5. Evaluating question. Is it a good idea to bring many tourists to a nature reserve? Give one argument for and one argument against.
  6. Practical question. You want to show Saryarka to a foreign guest. When is the best time to go, and what two rules must a visitor follow in a nature reserve?

Product: six short written answers in the notebook. Pairs share their answers to questions 4–6 aloud.

Extension for fast pairs: write one more question of any type for another pair.

Key for the teacher
  1. It is in the north of Kazakhstan. Steppe and lakes are protected there. (The answer is in the title of the video.)
  2. Not exactly. It consists of two protected areas: Naurzum State Nature Reserve and Korgalzhyn State Nature Reserve. (The text under the video names both.)
  3. The lakes lie on the birds’ long route to Siberia. The birds stop here to rest and to feed. (The text under the video calls the lakes stopover points.)
  4. Model answer: the birds would lose a place to rest and to find food, so many of them could not finish their journey. Any logical idea is right.
  5. Model answer: for, because tourists learn about nature and their money helps to protect it; against, because noise and litter can frighten the birds and animals. A good answer has two different arguments.
  6. Model answer: in spring or autumn, when the birds migrate. Rules: be quiet and keep your distance from the birds; do not leave litter. The Akmola Region page of the tourism portal says that birdwatchers come to the Korgalzhyn reserve mostly in spring and autumn. The two rules are the pupils’ own ideas.

Success criteria: the answers to questions 1–3 agree with the title and the text under the video. For questions 4–6 I accept any answer in English that uses information from the video or its description and gives a reason.

Local link: Naurzum is in Kostanay Region and Korgalzhyn is in Akmola Region.

Task 3. Fact or Opinion: Read and write if it is a fact or an opinion.

Link: Why Kazakhstan? (opens in a new tab) (Kazakhstan.travel, the national tourism portal)

Comprehension · pairs · 6 minutes · difficulty 3

Instruction for pupils:

Remember: a fact can be checked (a date, a number, a name, a place). An opinion shows what somebody thinks or feels, and you cannot prove it. Opinion words: best, amazing, beautiful, breathtaking, definitely, worth.

  1. Open the link and read the page “Why Kazakhstan?”. The tourism portal wants visitors to come, so it mixes facts and opinions.
  2. Read the ten statements. Write F (fact) or O (opinion) next to each one. For every fact, find the sentence on the page that proves it. In every opinion, underline the opinion word.
  3. Compare with your partner. If you do not agree, explain why.
  4. If you finish early, find two more opinion words on the page.
  1. Kazakhstan became an independent country in 1991.
  2. The greatest treasure of Kazakhstan is its people.
  3. Astana is the capital of Kazakhstan.
  4. The landscapes of Kazakhstan are breathtaking.
  5. There are mountains, steppes, deserts, canyons and lakes in Kazakhstan.
  6. People of many nationalities live in Kazakhstan.
  7. Kazakhstan is definitely worth visiting.
  8. Travellers can see medieval underground mosques and mausoleums in Kazakhstan.
  9. Kazakhstan is an amazing country for every traveller.
  10. There is a high-mountain skating rink in Almaty.

Product: ten statements labelled F or O. For each fact the pupil can show the sentence on the page that proves it. In each opinion the opinion word is underlined.

Key for the teacher

Facts: 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10. Opinions: 2, 4, 7, 9.

Opinion words: 2 “greatest treasure”; 4 “breathtaking”; 7 “definitely worth”; 9 “amazing”, “for every traveller”.

Success criteria: at least 8 of 10 answers are correct; the pupil can show where the page proves each fact and can name the opinion word in each opinion.

Discussion tip: the page calls the Almaty rink the highest in the world. This is not an opinion, because a height can be measured (1,691 m, see Task 4). It is a factual claim that we can check. “Breathtaking” cannot be measured. I found this claim only on the tourism portal, so statement 10 says “a high-mountain skating rink” and not “the highest”.

Task 4. Compare and Contrast: Read about Astana and Almaty and fill in the Venn diagram.

Links: page A Astana (opens in a new tab) and page B Almaty (opens in a new tab) (Kazakhstan.travel, the national tourism portal)

Comprehension · pairs · 7 minutes · difficulty 4

Instruction for pupils: work in pairs. Pupil A reads the page “Astana”. Pupil B reads the page “Almaty”.

  1. Read your page (3 minutes). Write four short notes and mark each one: ✓ = I knew this; + = this is new for me; − = I thought differently; ? = I want to know more.
  2. Tell your partner about your city. Use your notes and do not read the page aloud.
  3. Together draw a Venn diagram (two circles that cross). Write at least three points in each part: only Astana, both cities, only Almaty. Write facts, not opinions.
  4. Write two sentences about the cities: one with “Both ... and ...” and one with “but” or “while”. Then decide which city you would show to a foreign guest first, and why.

Product: a Venn diagram with at least three facts in each of its three parts, two comparing sentences and an oral choice of a city with one reason.

Key for the teacher

Model sentences: “Both Astana and Almaty have shopping malls, restaurants and interesting architecture.” “Astana stands on the Ishim River, while Almaty is surrounded by snow-capped mountains.”

Success criteria: at least three facts in each part; no opinions in the diagram; two correct comparing sentences; a choice of a city with one reason. I accept any other fact that is really on the two pages.

The nine model points can be given to weaker or slower pairs as nine cards to sort. This also saves time.

I do not accept as facts two advertising claims of the Astana page: that Astana is the youngest capital in the world and that Hazrat Sultan is the largest mosque in Central Asia (see the notes for the teacher).

Task 5. Three Days in Kazakhstan: Plan a route for a foreign guest, write a blog post and give a pitch.

Link: Regions of Kazakhstan (opens in a new tab) (Kazakhstan.travel, the national tourism portal)

Reflection · groups of 3–4 · 17 minutes · difficulty 5

Instruction for pupils: work in groups of three or four. A guest from another country is coming to Kazakhstan for only three days and knows nothing about it. You are the travel bloggers who plan the trip.

  • Navigator chooses the places.
  • Fact-checker finds and checks the facts.
  • Writer writes the post.
  • Speaker leads the pitch.

In a group of three, one pupil takes two roles.

  1. Open the link. Choose two or three places (cities or regions) and read what a tourist can see there.
  2. Decide together where the guest will go on Day 1, Day 2 and Day 3. Check that your plan is possible in three days. Remember how big the country is.
  3. Write a blog post of 100–120 words with the title “Three days in Kazakhstan”. Use at least three facts from Tasks 1–4 and underline them. Add one opinion and mark it clearly: start the sentence with “In our opinion, ...” and give a reason.
  4. Prepare a 2-minute pitch for the class. Show your route and explain why it is the best. Every member of the group says at least one sentence. After each pitch the other groups ask one question.

Product: a group blog post of 100–120 words and a 2-minute group pitch, followed by one question from the class.

Success criteria for pupils:

  • Route: three days, two or three places, and the group can explain how the guest travels.
  • Blog post: it has a title and 100–120 words.
  • Facts: at least three correct facts from Tasks 1–4, underlined.
  • Opinion: exactly one; it starts with “In our opinion, ...” and has a reason.
  • Pitch: not longer than two minutes, every member speaks, and the group answers one question.

The blog post and the pitch are assessed with the rubrics from Task 4 of this portfolio.

Key for the teacher

This task has no single right answer. I check the product against the success criteria above.

Model post (116 words without the title; every fact comes from the pages of Tasks 1–4; in the pupils’ posts the facts are underlined):

Three days in Kazakhstan

Day 1: Astana. Our guest starts in the capital. We go up Bayterek Tower: its viewing platform is 97 metres high. In the evening we walk along the Ishim River.

Day 2: Korgalzhyn. This nature reserve is part of Saryarka, a UNESCO World Heritage site. Many migratory birds stop at its lakes in spring and autumn.

Day 3: Almaty. We take a plane because Kazakhstan is the ninth largest country in the world. We skate at Medeu, a rink 1,691 metres above sea level, and ride the cable car to Kok Tobe.

In our opinion, this is the best route for a first visit because the guest sees a modern city, wild nature and high mountains.

Closing reflection (3 minutes): pupils return to the table from Task 1 and finish the sentence “I thought that ..., but now I know that ...”. Then the class votes for the route they would really choose.

Notes for the teacher

  • Watch the video before the lesson. I confirmed the title, the channel, the length and the text under the video. I have not checked the narration and the pictures yet. The keys for questions 1–3 are built only on the title and on that text, so before the lesson I have to watch the three minutes once, adjust the key to what is really said and shown, and add the birds and animals that appear.
  • Set the English audio track. YouTube offers auto-dubbed audio tracks for this video. Russian is one of them, and the player may switch to a dub by itself. Before the lesson I choose Settings, Audio track, English (original) and check that YouTube opens on the school network. If it does not, I show the video once from my own computer.
  • The text under the video is from 2013. It calls the saiga antelope critically endangered. Since December 2023 the IUCN lists it as near threatened (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service). I can use this for a short “check the date of your source” moment.

Assignment

Prepare 2–3 tasks for gifted and talented children of the 5th–7th form. Skill: reading. Find any text from a real textbook for the 5th–7th form and elaborate 2–3 tasks based on the text.

Aim

My work

The text

TextbookEnglish Plus. Grade 6. Student’s Book (Kazakhstan edition). Authors: Ben Wetz, Diana Pye

It compares a few attacks by sharks and crocodiles with the millions of animals that people kill for food, fur or fun. It names animals from Kazakhstan’s red list, explains the danger from pollution and construction, and asks readers to change.

Where the tasks stand in the lesson

In the lecture, ABC planning means three levels of tasks: A is a task for everybody, B is a task that some students can do, and C is a task that certain students can do. I planned my three tasks as level C. The class does the ordinary exercises on page 34 (levels A and B), and at the same time a gifted pupil works on one of these tasks. Task 1

Fact detective

Type from the lecture

Research activity and problematic learning from the slide “Forms of work with gifted and talented”. It is close to N. Timurzieva’s example for the 8th–11th forms, “Compare texts and present the differences”, but I made it simpler for the 6th form.

Instruction for the pupil

The article “Danger! Humans!” gives many facts. Can we trust them? Check.

  1. Read the article again. Find three facts you can check: numbers (sharks, crocodiles, chairs) or the list of red-list animals. Write them on your card in your own words.
  2. Look carefully: does the writer say where the facts come from? Circle YES or NO.
  3. Choose one fact. Check it in two other sources. A source is a book or a website with information. Use an encyclopedia, a website your teacher gives you, or page 40 of your Student’s Book for the red list. You can read in Kazakh or Russian, but write in English.
  4. Finish the sentences:
    • The article says …
    • Source 1 (name: …) says …
    • Source 2 (name: …) says …
    • The facts are: the same / a little different / very different.
  5. Think and write 2–3 sentences. Why can the facts be different? Is the writer’s main idea still true? Why?
  6. Tell the class about your card in one minute.
Product

A one-page fact-check card and a one-minute report to the class.

Time

25 minutes. If there is no internet in class, steps 3–5 are homework.

How the teacher checks

a checklist, 1 point for each line, 5 points in total.

  • Three facts are taken from the article and reported correctly.
  • Two sources are named.
  • The verdict matches what the sources say.
  • The conclusion gives a reason and says something about the writer’s main idea.
  • The report is clear.

A different number in another source is not the pupil’s mistake. Figures in different sources can differ, and noticing this is the aim of the task.

Why it suits gifted pupils

the pupil goes from understanding the text to evaluating it. He or she questions a printed text, compares it with other sources and decides if the main idea is still true. The lecture says that children with high aptitudes understand the gist of the problem, prefer complexity and know how to study. The task has no single right answer, only an answer with good reasons. Among the types of genius (giftedness) in the lecture it is closest to the intellectual and the academic type.

Task 2

Who am I? A red-list story-riddle

Type from the lecture

“Make a story-riddle”, one of N. Timurzieva’s examples for the 5th–7th forms. It is a creative task.

Instruction for the pupil
  1. Read the last paragraph of the article again. Choose one animal from Kazakhstan’s red list: a horse, a leopard, a wild dog, a lynx, an otter, a bat or a bear. Keep it secret.
  2. Find three facts about this animal in Kazakhstan: where it lives, what it eats, why it is in danger. Use books or the internet.
  3. Write a story-riddle of 6–8 sentences. Write as the animal: “I …”. Do not write the animal’s name. Your riddle must have:
    • the place where you live;
    • two sentences about what you are doing right now (present continuous: “I am hiding …”);
    • one reason from the article why you are in danger, with two of these words: hunt, fur, pollution, construction, habitat, extinct;
    • the last line: “Who am I?”
  4. Read your riddle to the class. Your classmates guess the animal and say which clues helped them.
Product

A story-riddle of 6–8 sentences written in the first person and read aloud to the class. The pupil may draw the answer on the back of the sheet.

Time

25 minutes. The search for facts in step 2 can be homework.

How the teacher checks

a checklist, 1 point for each line, 5 points in total. Small language mistakes that do not block the meaning do not lower the mark.

  • The riddle has 6–8 sentences and no animal name.
  • It has two correct sentences in the present continuous.
  • The reason for the danger really comes from the article and has two of the target words.
  • The facts are true: the pupil shows the notes and names the source.
  • The classmates can guess the animal from the clues.
Why it suits gifted pupils

the pupil turns information from the article and from his or her own reading into a new genre and a new point of view, the animal’s. The clues must be not too easy and not too hard. The grammar of the unit (the present continuous on page 35) and the new words are used in a new situation. The lecture names both features for a child with aptitudes: an original performance and the use of skills in different situations. The type of giftedness is creativity.

Task 3

You are the teacher: an A-B-C quiz

Type from the lecture

“Prepare the control questions for others”, one of N. Timurzieva’s examples for the 5th–7th forms. I built it as a multi-level task: the pupil uses the idea of ABC planning for his or her own questions. The level C questions are close to her example for the 8th–11th forms, “Make up your own philosophic questions on the text”.

Instruction for the pupil

Make a quiz about the article for your classmates.

  1. Write six questions. Do not copy questions from the book.
    • A, two questions for everybody: the answer is in one sentence of the article. Start with What, Which or How many.
    • B, two questions for some classmates: they must put two pieces of information together or explain something. Start with Why or How.
    • C, two questions for thinkers: the answer is not in the article. Your classmates must think and give a reason. Start with “Do you agree that …?”, “What can we do …?” or “What will happen if …?”
  2. Write an answer key. For A and B answers, write the number of the paragraph. For C questions, write one good answer.
  3. Give your quiz to three or four classmates. Check their answers. Tell them which answer was the best and why.
Product

A quiz card with six questions on three levels and an answer key, and a short quiz that the pupil runs in a group of three or four classmates.

Time

20 minutes.

How the teacher checks

the teacher reads the card before the quiz and gives up to 6 points.

  • The two A questions fit their level: the answer is in one sentence (1 point).
  • The two B questions fit their level: the answer needs linking or explaining (1 point).
  • The two C questions fit their level: an opinion with a reason, not in the text (1 point).
  • The answer key is correct and has the paragraph numbers (1 point).
  • The question forms are correct (1 point).
  • The questions do not repeat the questions of the textbook (1 point).

During the quiz the teacher watches whether the author explains the answers or only says “right” and “wrong”.

Why it suits gifted pupils

writing a good question needs a deeper understanding of the text than answering one. To sort the questions into three levels the pupil has to think about how people think. The lecture names both features for children with high aptitudes: organised thinking and knowing how to study. The pupil also takes the role of the teacher and explains answers to others. This supports the social (leadership) type of giftedness.

Essay on underachieving

Assignment

Write an essay of 200–250 words on one of three topics: “The reasons of underachievement of pupils”, “Underachievement of pupils: parents or teachers?” or “My advice of work with underachievers”.

My choice: the third topic, because advice is something I can use in a real lesson.

Aim

To form my own position on underachievement and to turn the ideas of the lecture into practical advice for my future lessons.

My work

My Advice on Working with Underachievers

Every class has pupils who learn more slowly than the others. In the lecture I was surprised to hear that Isaac Newton and Thomas Edison were also called weak pupils at school. This fact changed my view: an underachiever is not a lazy child, but a child whose potential has not been discovered yet. So my first tip is simple: do not put a label on the pupil.

My second tip is to look for the reason. It can be external, like the influence of the family or the street, or internal, like health problems or a short memory span. Different reasons need different help, so I would start with observation and a personal talk.

Thirdly, I would create a situation of success in every lesson. A weak pupil needs a task that he or she can complete, and a phrase that gives confidence: “You will manage it” or “This part is done well”. A small victory motivates more than ten remarks.

Finally, I would change my own teaching. Underachievers need more time to think, a variety of activities and visual support such as pictures, tables and plans. I would also teach them how to learn, for example how to work with a dictionary.

Leo Tolstoy wrote that the easier it is for the teacher to teach, the harder it is for the child to learn. I agree with him. Working with underachievers is extra work for the teacher, but it is exactly the work that makes us teachers.

How the essay is built

  1. No labels · great people were “weak pupils” too
  2. Find the reason · external or internal
  3. Situation of success · a task the pupil can do and a supporting phrase
  4. Change the teaching · time, variety, visual support

What I learned

This essay made me sort the ideas of the lecture by one question: what can I do in class tomorrow? I practised keeping to a word limit and giving a reason for each piece of advice. The idea I will keep is the situation of success. It costs the teacher one sentence, but for a weak pupil it can change the whole lesson.

Assignment

Memo on Lesson Study.

Aim

To put the main points of Lesson Study on one page, so that I can look at it before I plan or observe a research lesson with colleagues.

My work

I understand a memo as a reminder card: one page, short phrases, no long text. I took the content from the lecture and checked it against the handbook by Pete Dudley (2011). The year 1999 for the English term comes from Lewis (2002), who cites Yoshida’s dissertation.

Memo

Lesson Study

What is it?

Classroom action research. A small group of teachers plans, teaches, observes and analyses research lessons together (Dudley, 2011).

Where is it from?

  • Japan, in use since the 1870s.
  • Japanese term: 授業研究 (jugyo kenkyu).
  • English term “lesson study”: Makoto Yoshida, 1999.

The golden rule

The lesson belongs to the group. We discuss the pupils’ learning, not the teacher.

The cycle (Dudley’s model)

  1. Form a group. About three teachers agree what to improve in the pupils’ learning.
  2. Plan together. Plan the research lesson and predict what each case pupil will do.
  3. Teach and observe. One teaches, the others watch the case pupils.
  4. Interview case pupils. Straight after the lesson, five minutes at most.
  5. Discuss. Within 24 hours: case pupils first, the teaching last.
  6. Repeat and share. At least three research lessons, then present the findings to colleagues.

Three case pupils

  • Higher attaining
  • Middle attaining
  • Lower attaining

About three. Each one stands for a group of learners in the class.

If you observe

Do
  • Watch your case pupil.
  • Note what the pupil really does.
  • Compare it with the prediction.
Do not
  • Judge the teacher.
  • Start the discussion with the teaching.
  • Wait more than 24 hours to discuss.

Four assets (from the lecture)

  • Natural classroom conditions
  • Open process
  • Systemic character
  • Everything is recorded

Three styles (from the lecture)

  • Reproductive
  • Creative
  • Emotional and value-oriented

Remember

Plan together. Teach and observe. Analyse. Improve.

Sources: Dudley, P. (2011). Lesson Study: a handbook. Cambridge: Lesson Study UK. lessonstudy.co.uk. William Paterson University, Center for Lesson Study (n.d.). Makoto Yoshida: short biography. wpunj.edu. Lewis, C. (2002). Does lesson study have a future in the United States? Nagoya Journal of Education and Human Development, 1, 1–23. Author’s manuscript: lessonresearch.net. The four assets and the three styles are from the course lecture.

What I learned

In my first draft, which I wrote from my lecture notes, Lesson Study began in Japan in the 1970s. When I checked this date in Dudley’s handbook, I saw that it has been used there since the 1870s, so I corrected it. I also learned that three research lessons, case pupils and pupil interviews belong to Dudley’s model and are not a rule for every country. The idea I will keep is the golden rule: after the lesson we talk about what the pupils learned, and nobody gives marks to the teacher.

What I learned

I learned how a published test is built. It has statements, rules for scoring and levels that explain the result. Not every test gives points: the first one has a key and one number, and the second one asks the teacher to choose a level and to write the next steps. I also understood that a test result is a starting point for observation and a conversation, not a label on a pupil or a teacher. Now, before I use a test, I check who the author is and where the test was published.

Module 7 · Task 9 of 10

Questionnaire “Inclusive education”

Assignment

Making up a questionnaire on the topic “Inclusive education”.

Aim

To find out what future teachers and teachers around me know about inclusive education, how they feel about it and what they see as the main barriers.

My work

The questionnaire has ten questions in four parts: about the respondent, knowledge, attitude and readiness, and barriers. It is anonymous and takes about three minutes. In the online form every question is given in English and in Russian.

Online form: Inclusive education / Инклюзивное образование (Google Forms)

  1. Single choice

    Who are you?

    • A student, future teacher
    • A school teacher
    • A college or university teacher
    • Other
  2. Single choice

    How well do you know what “inclusive education” means?

    • I can explain it to other people
    • I know it in general
    • I have only heard the term
    • I do not know
  3. Single choice

    Have you ever studied or worked in a class with a child with special educational needs?

    • Yes, I studied in such a class
    • Yes, I taught such a class
    • Yes, both
    • No
    • I am not sure
  4. Scale 1–5

    “Children with special educational needs should study in mainstream classes together with other children.”

    1 = strongly disagree … 5 = strongly agree

    12345

    “Pupils without special educational needs also benefit from studying in an inclusive class.”

    1 = strongly disagree … 5 = strongly agree

    12345
  6. Scale 1–5

    How ready do you feel to teach in an inclusive class?

    1 = not ready at all … 5 = fully ready

    12345
  7. Multiple choice

    What would help a teacher most in an inclusive class? Choose up to three.

    • Special training courses
    • A teacher’s assistant in the classroom
    • Fewer pupils in the class
    • Adapted textbooks and materials
    • Support from a psychologist or a special education teacher

  8. Single choice

    What is the main barrier to inclusive education in our schools today?

    • Not enough assistants and specialists
    • Buildings and equipment are not adapted

    • Too many pupils in one class
    • Other
    • I do not know
  9. Multiple choice

    Which activities in a foreign language lesson suit an inclusive class best? Choose all that apply.

    • Pair and group work
    • Games and songs
    • Visual materials: pictures, cards, schemes
    • Individual tasks of different levels
    • Digital tools and apps
  10. Open

    In one or two sentences: what would you change first in inclusive education in our schools?

Why the questionnaire is built this way

  • Easy questions first, the open question last.
  • One idea in one question and no leading words.
  • A five-point scale in questions 4–6: agreement in questions 4 and 5, readiness in question 6.
  • Question 9 connects inclusion with my subject and with other modules of the course: group work, different levels of tasks and ICT.

What I learned

I learned that a good questionnaire starts from the question “What do I want to find out?” and not from the questions themselves. I practised three types of questions: closed, scale and open. I also saw how easy it is to push a respondent to the answer I want, so I rewrote several statements in neutral words.

Module 7 · Task 10 of 10

Review of the questionnaire results

Assignment

Writing a short review – result of the questionnaire.

Aim

To learn to read the answers of real respondents, to show them in a clear form and to make careful conclusions from a small sample.

My work

This is a pilot survey. I sent the online form from Task 9 to a small group: half of the respondents were future teachers and half were people of other professions. The answers are anonymous. A group of this size shows tendencies, not statistics, so I describe the results in words and in average scores.

Knowledge and experience

  • Half of the respondents know the term “inclusive education” in general. One person can explain it to others. A third do not know what it means.
  • Half have taught a class with a child with special educational needs. A third have never been in such a class.

Attitude and readiness: average scores from 1 to 5

2.7Children with special educational needs should study in mainstream classes
3.5Pupils without special needs also benefit from an inclusive class
2.8I feel ready to teach in an inclusive class

The answers about readiness are divided: half of the respondents chose 1 or 2, and a third chose 5. Nobody chose 4.

What would help a teacher most

  • Adapted textbooks and materialstwo thirds
  • Special training courseshalf
  • A teacher's assistant in the classroomhalf
  • Fewer pupils in the classhalf
  • An accessible building and equipmenthalf
  • Support from a psychologist or a special education teacherone answer
  • Exchange of experience with colleaguesone answer

Barriers and activities

  • Two barriers were named equally often, each by a third of the respondents: not enough trained teachers, and the attitude of parents and pupils. Nobody chose buildings or the number of pupils as the main barrier.
  • For a foreign language lesson the respondents most often chose pair and group work and visual materials (half each). Digital tools were chosen least often.
  • In the open question two people gave a concrete answer: to change the attitude of teachers to children with special needs, and to give more grants for special training of future teachers. The others wrote that they did not know.

My conclusions

  1. People around me have heard about inclusion, but only a few can explain it. The term is known better than the idea.
  2. The attitude is careful. The respondents agree more that an inclusive class is useful for other pupils (3.5) than that children with special needs should study in mainstream classes (2.7).
  3. Readiness is low for half of the group. The help they ask for is practical: adapted materials, training and an assistant.
  4. The main barriers are about people, not about buildings: trained teachers and attitude.

Limits of the survey: the group is small and mixed, and no working school teacher took part. For a real study I would send the form to teachers of several schools and compare their answers with the answers of students.

What I learned

I learned that collecting answers is harder than writing questions: people answer slowly, and not everyone who answers belongs to the group I planned. I also learned to be careful with conclusions. With a small group I can speak about tendencies only, so I wrote “half” and “a third” and gave the limits of the survey openly. The most interesting result for me was the gap between two attitudes: people accept the benefit of inclusion more easily than inclusion itself.
