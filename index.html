Module 1 · Task 1 of 10

Interview with a teacher

Assignment

Interview with… Think of 5 questions on the use of the group work. Ask these questions to a really working teacher and present the result in the form of a report.

Aim

To learn from an experienced teacher how group work really goes in a language classroom: how groups are formed, what goes wrong and how the result is evaluated.

My work

I prepared five questions. Each of them is connected with one part of the lecture: the place of group work in a lesson, forming groups and roles, drawbacks, evaluation and examples.

I did not want a list of textbook answers, so I asked about real lessons: what was done, with which forms and what went wrong. Below is the report of our talk. I translated the answers from Russian.

  • 10–15 minutes of group work in almost every lesson
  • 4–5 pupils in a mixed group
  • 4 changing roles: speaker, secretary, timekeeper, language keeper
  • 3 views in evaluation: teacher, other groups, the pupil

The rules hung on the board, and when somebody broke one, it was enough to point at it.

  1. How often did you use group work in your lessons, and at which stage of the lesson?

    Group work was in almost every lesson and usually took 10–15 minutes. In the 5th–7th forms it was mostly at the stage of work with a text: the groups read, answered questions and looked for the necessary information. In the 9th form it was at the stage of consolidation, in the form of a quest, where the groups moved from station to station and did tasks on the topic we had studied. At the end of the lesson group work helped to sum up and to reflect.

  2. How did you form the groups and share the roles?

    To share the roles in the 9th–11th forms we used short leadership tests. They showed who was ready to lead and who would do better as an executor or a secretary. After that we made mixed groups of four or five pupils, and each group had a pupil with leadership qualities. The roles of the speaker, the secretary, the timekeeper and the “language keeper” changed from task to task, so that pupils tried different positions and not only the usual one.

  3. What difficulties did you meet, for example passive or dominating pupils or noise, and how did you solve them?

    The main difficulties were passive pupils, dominating leaders and noise, especially in the senior forms. So in the 10th–11th forms the pupils and the teacher wrote the rules of group work together at the very beginning: speak in turn, listen to each other, do your role and speak the foreign language. The rules hung on the board, and when somebody broke one, it was enough to point at it. Passive pupils got a concrete part of the task. Active leaders became timekeepers, so that they organised the work and did not do everything themselves.

  4. How did you evaluate the work of the group and the contribution of each pupil?

    In the 9th form the quest was assessed with rubrics, which the pupils received in advance: understanding of the task, accuracy of the language, teamwork and presentation of the result. The work of a group was evaluated by the teacher and by the other groups. The contribution of each pupil was seen through observation and self-evaluation inside the group, so it was clear who really took part. After group work there was a short individual task to check that everyone had understood the topic.

  5. Which group task worked best in your French or Kazakh lessons, and what would you advise a beginning teacher?

    The best one was the quest in the 9th form. The groups went through stations with reading, vocabulary and speaking tasks and got a clue to the next stage. The pupils were involved because the task had a goal and an element of competition, and the rubrics made the assessment fair and clear. My advice to a beginning teacher is to agree on the rules of group work with the class in advance and to give clear instructions with a time limit. It is also useful to start with simple tasks, for example work with a text in the junior forms, and to move step by step to complex formats like quests.

My conclusions

  • Group work is a regular short part of a lesson (10–15 minutes), not a rare event.
  • The typical problems are the same as in the lecture: dominating pupils, passive pupils and noise. They are solved before the task starts, with rules and roles.
  • Fair evaluation needs three views: the teacher, the other groups and the pupil.

What I learned

While preparing the questions I learned to ask about practice, not about theory: a good question starts with “how” and asks for an example. I also saw that five questions are enough if each one covers a different side of group work.

The answers showed me that the other tasks of this portfolio are connected in real practice: rules, roles, leadership tests, a quest and rubrics work together in one lesson. The advice I will follow first is to agree on the rules with the class before the first group task.

Module 1 · Task 2 of 10

Rules for the group work

Assignment

Elaborate the rules for the group work for the pupils of the 10th–11th form (7–10 rules).

Aim

To give senior pupils a short and clear code that makes group work fair and productive, and to practise presenting classroom rules in a visual form.

My work

I wrote eight rules and hid them in the word TEAMWORK, so that pupils can remember them easily. Each letter opens one rule.

  1. T

    Take a role

    Everyone has a job: manager, writer, speaker or critic. We change roles next time.

  2. E

    Everyone contributes

    No passengers. Each of us gives at least one idea before the group decides.

  3. A

    Argue with ideas, not with people

    We can disagree, but politely and with a reason: “I see it differently, because…”

  4. M

    Mind the time

    The manager watches the clock. We plan the minutes and finish on time.

  5. W

    We speak English

    The task is our chance to practise. If we slip into another language, we switch back.

  6. O

    One voice at a time

    We listen to the end and do not interrupt. Quiet members are asked first.

  7. R

    Respect every opinion

    No idea is laughed at. A strange idea today can be the best one tomorrow.

  8. K

    Keep the goal in sight

    We check the result against the task and finish with a short self- and peer evaluation.

What I learned

While writing the rules I understood that most problems of group work, such as the dominating pupil or the lazy one, can be prevented before the task starts. Rules work better when they are short, positive and agreed with the class, not just given by the teacher. I also practised turning a list into a visual product that senior pupils will actually read.

Module 3 · Task 4 of 10

Rubrics for assessment

Assignment

Rubrics for assessment (2 types of the work): create a holistic rubric and an analytic rubric.

Aim

To learn to describe the quality of pupils' work in clear levels, so that a mark is transparent for the pupil and quicker to give for the teacher.

My work

Both rubrics assess the final products of my quest for the 9th form (Task 3): a written blog post and a two-minute group pitch.

Analytic rubric · blog post “Three days in Kazakhstan: our route” (100–120 words)

Criteria50–59 %60–69 %70–79 %80–100 %
Task completion Mentions one or two places; the three-day route is not clear; fewer than 80 words. Describes a route, but one day is missing or very short; 80–99 words. Describes all three days; one detail (transport, time or reason) is missing; 100–120 words. Describes all three days with places, activities and reasons; keeps to 100–120 words.
Facts and opinion Gives no checked facts or mixes facts with opinions. Uses one or two facts; the opinion is not marked. Uses three facts from the quest; the opinion is present but not clearly marked. Uses at least three accurate facts from the quest and one clearly marked opinion supported by a reason.
Organisation Sentences are not connected; there is no beginning or ending. Some order, but linking words are rare; the ending is missing. Clear order by days with some linking words (first, then, finally). Logical order by days, an opening hook, a closing sentence and a variety of linking words.
Vocabulary Very basic words with many repetitions; topic words are not used. A few topic words (steppe, landmark, route); some are used wrongly. A good range of topic words with rare mistakes. A wide range of topic words and adjectives used accurately and naturally.
Grammar and spelling Frequent errors make the text hard to understand. Several errors in tenses or spelling, but the meaning is clear. A few errors that do not prevent understanding. Almost no errors; future forms and modal verbs are used correctly.

Holistic rubric · group pitch “Three days in Kazakhstan” (2 minutes)

LevelDescription
A · ExcellentThe group presents a complete and convincing route within two minutes. Every member speaks. The speech is clear and mostly fluent, with accurate topic vocabulary; the facts are correct and the group's opinion is supported by reasons. The group answers a question from the audience confidently.
B · GoodThe route is complete and clear, but some reasons are missing. Most members speak. There are some pauses and language mistakes, but they do not prevent understanding. The group can answer a simple question.
C · SatisfactoryThe route is presented only in part, or the time limit is not kept. One or two members do most of the talking, the others read from notes. Frequent mistakes and long pauses make some parts hard to follow. Answers to questions are short or unclear.
D · Needs improvementThe route is unclear or incomplete. The speech is read aloud or given by one member only. Mistakes and pauses prevent understanding. The group cannot answer questions.

What I learned

I learned the practical difference between the two types. A holistic rubric gives one general level and is fast, so it suits a short oral answer. An analytic rubric separates the criteria and shows the pupil exactly what to improve, so it suits written work. The hardest part was to describe each level with observable facts instead of words like “good” or “bad”.

Module 5 · Task 6 of 10

Essay on underachieving

Assignment

Write an essay of 200–250 words on one of three topics: “The reasons of underachievement of pupils”, “Underachievement of pupils: parents or teachers?” or “My advice of work with underachievers”.

My choice: the third topic, because advice is something I can use in a real lesson.

Aim

To form my own position on underachievement and to turn the ideas of the lecture into practical advice for my future lessons.

My work

My Advice on Working with Underachievers

Every class has pupils who learn more slowly than the others. At the lecture I was surprised to hear that Isaac Newton and Thomas Edison were also called weak pupils at school. This fact changed my view: an underachiever is not a lazy child, but a child whose potential has not been opened yet. So my first advice is simple: do not put a label on the pupil.

My second advice is to look for the reason. It can be external, like the influence of the family or the street, or internal, like health problems or a short memory span. Different reasons need different help, so I would start with observation and a personal talk.

Thirdly, I would create a situation of success in every lesson. A weak pupil needs a task that he or she can complete, and a phrase that gives confidence: “You will manage it” or “This part is done well”. A small victory motivates more than ten remarks.

Finally, I would change my own teaching. Underachievers need more time to think, a variety of activities and visual support such as pictures, tables and plans. I would also teach them how to learn, for example how to work with a dictionary.

Leo Tolstoy wrote that the easier it is for the teacher to teach, the harder it is for the child to learn. I agree with him. Working with underachievers is extra work for the teacher, but it is exactly the work that makes us teachers.

How the essay is built

  1. No labels · great people were “weak pupils” too
  2. Find the reason · external or internal
  3. Situation of success · a task the pupil can do and a supporting phrase
  4. Change the teaching · time, variety, visual support

What I learned

This essay made me sort the ideas of the lecture by one question: what can I do in class tomorrow? I practised keeping to a word limit and giving a reason for each piece of advice. The idea I will keep is the situation of success. It costs the teacher one sentence, but for a weak pupil it can change the whole lesson.

Module 7 · Task 8 of 10

Two tests for defining leaders

Assignment

Two tests for defining leaders.

Aim

To have two quick tools for the classroom. The first shows whether a pupil has leadership potential. The second shows what kind of leader he or she is.

My work

I made both tests myself on the basis of the lecture. They are for pupils of the 9th–11th form and take about five minutes each. You can try them right here: choose the answers and press the button.

Test 1

Five keys of a leader

Self-assessment · 15 statements · 5 minutes

What it shows: the level of leadership potential and the strongest and the weakest of five qualities. In the lecture these qualities are named as the ways of gaining leadership: sensitivity to people, the ability to define a goal, an optimistic outlook, communication and initiative.

Instruction for the pupil: read each statement and choose how often it is true about you. There are no right or wrong answers.

0 · Never1 · Sometimes2 · Often3 · Always

Sensitivity to people
  1. I notice when a classmate is upset, even if he or she says nothing.
  2. Before I decide something for the group, I ask what the others think.
  3. I can feel when the mood in the group changes.
Goal setting
  1. When we get a task, I quickly understand what result we need.
  2. I can divide a big task into small steps.
  3. I keep working for the goal even when it becomes boring.
Optimism
  1. When something goes wrong, I look for a way out, not for the guilty.
  2. I believe that our group can do the task well.
  3. I can cheer up my classmates when they lose heart.
Communication
  1. I can explain my idea so that everyone understands it.
  2. It is easy for me to talk to classmates I do not know well.
  3. In an argument I can help two sides to agree.
Initiative
  1. I am usually the first to suggest what to do.
  2. I take responsibility if the group makes a mistake.
  3. I offer my help without waiting to be asked.

Key and interpretation

Never = 0, Sometimes = 1, Often = 2, Always = 3. The maximum is 45 points, or 9 points for each key.

PointsLevelWhat it means for the teacher
31–45Clear leaderThe pupil is ready to take responsibility. Give him or her the role of the manager, but also teach to listen to the quiet members.
16–30Situational leaderThe pupil leads when the task is close to him or her. Offer the leading role in the topics he or she is good at.
0–15Team player for nowLeadership is not shown yet, but it can be developed. Start with small roles: the writer or the speaker of the group.

The key with the lowest score shows which quality to train first.

Test 2

What kind of leader are you?

Situational test · 8 situations · 5 minutes

What it shows: the style of a leader. The lecture divides leaders by the style of activity into authoritarian, democratic and liberal.

Instruction for the pupil: imagine that you are the manager of a group. For each situation choose the answer that is closest to what you would really do.

  1. Your group gets a project and one week to do it. What do you do first?

    • I share the tasks myself: I know who does what best.
    • I ask everyone what they want to do, and we decide together.
    • I let everyone take any part. It will work out by itself.

  2. Two members of your group cannot agree.

    • I listen to both and help them find a common decision.
    • I wait. They will sort it out themselves.
    • I decide who is right, and we move on.

  3. One member of the group does nothing.

    • I do not interfere. It is his or her choice.
    • I say exactly what to do and check it later.
    • I ask what the problem is and offer a task he or she likes.

  4. The deadline is tomorrow and the work is not ready.

    • I take control and tell everyone what to finish tonight.
    • We meet quickly, see what is left and share it fairly.
    • Everyone knows the deadline. Each will do what he or she can.

  5. A member suggests an idea that you do not like.

    • I say: “Do as you wish.”
    • I ask the group to discuss it and to vote.
    • I say no. We have no time for experiments.

  6. The group has done the work very well. Who presents it?

    • We decide together who presents, and we thank everyone.
    • I present it myself, because I am responsible for the result.
    • Anyone who wants to can present it.

  7. The teacher criticises the work of your group.

    • I find who made the mistake and ask this person to correct it.
    • I think it is not so important. Next time it will be better.
    • We analyse together what went wrong and how to improve it.

  8. What is the main thing for a leader?

    • Freedom for every member.
    • Discipline and a result on time.
    • A team where everyone is heard.

Key and interpretation

Count which style you chose most often. If two styles have the same number, the pupil mixes them.

StyleStrong sideRisk
AuthoritarianFast decisions and order. Useful when time is short.The others stop thinking and only follow. In the lecture this pole is called tyranny.
DemocraticEveryone is heard, so the group supports the decision.Decisions take more time. The leader has to stop the discussion at the right moment.
LiberalFreedom and trust. It works in a strong, motivated group.Nobody is responsible for the result. This is the other pole: absence of initiative.

Answer key (A = authoritarian, D = democratic, L = liberal; the order of the options in each situation): 1 A D L · 2 D L A · 3 L A D · 4 A D L · 5 L D A · 6 D A L · 7 A L D · 8 L A D

How I would use the tests

  • Test 1 before a long project, to choose the managers of the groups and to see who needs a small first role.
  • Test 2 after the project, as a reflection for the managers: “Was my style good for my group?”
  • Together with observation and J. Moreno's sociometry from the lecture. A self-assessment shows how a pupil sees himself or herself. Sociometry shows whom the class really chooses.

Both tests are my own express tools based on the lecture (the ways of gaining leadership and B. Parygin's classification of leaders by style). They are not standardised psychological tests, so I would use the results to start a conversation, not to put a label on a pupil.

What I learned

I learned that a leader is not one type of person. The lecture names leaders by activity, by content and by style, and each type is useful in its own situation. To write the statements I had to turn abstract qualities into behaviour that a teenager can recognise in himself or herself. I also understood the limit of any test: it is only the first step, and the teacher has to check the result in real group work.

Why the questionnaire is built this way

  • Easy questions first, the open question last.
  • One idea in one question and no leading words.
  • The same five-point scale in questions 4–6, so that the answers can be compared.
  • Question 9 connects inclusion with my subject and with other modules of the course: group work, different levels of tasks and ICT.

What I learned

I learned that a good questionnaire starts from the question “What do I want to find out?” and not from the questions themselves. I practised three types of questions: closed, scale and open. I also saw how easy it is to push a respondent to the answer I want, so I rewrote several statements in neutral words.
